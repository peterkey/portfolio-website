# Domain Pitfalls: IT Portfolio Improvement

**Domain:** IT professional portfolio — copywriting, hero section, design, credibility
**Researched:** 2026-03-09
**Confidence:** HIGH (direct codebase review + established copywriting/hiring-funnel principles)

---

## Critical Pitfalls

Mistakes that cause the redesign to fail at its core goal — hooking a recruiter or hiring manager in under 10 seconds.

---

### Pitfall 1: The Hero Describes the Candidate, Not the Value

**What goes wrong:** The hero copy reads like a job description rather than a value proposition. It says what the person does, not what they deliver for the employer. The recruiter mentally places them in a pile rather than a shortlist.

**Current example in this codebase:**
> "Customer-focused IT professional delivering first-line technical support in high-volume environments. Skilled at resolving Microsoft 365, Windows OS, and Active Directory issues within minutes."

This is accurate. It is also identical to how every IT support CV reads. There is no hook. "Customer-focused" and "high-volume environments" are cargo-cult phrases that appear on 90% of support CVs. The reader's eye glides over them.

**Why it happens:** The candidate knows their work well and defaults to describing it accurately. Accuracy and persuasion are different jobs. Description answers "what do you do?" — persuasion answers "why should I care?"

**Consequences:** Visitors whose attention spans are already strained (recruiters scanning 50+ portfolios) move on without forming a distinct impression. The beautiful NOC design attracts the eye but the copy fails to hold the mind.

**Prevention:**
- Lead with outcome, not activity. "Keeps systems running so teams don't notice IT exists" is more memorable than "provides first-line technical support."
- Use one concrete, specific claim that is hard to fake: uptime figure, resolution time, scale (users, sites, devices).
- The value proposition should answer: "Why hire this person over the ten others with similar CVs?"

**Warning signs:**
- Hero paragraph contains "customer-focused," "high-volume," "team player," or "passionate about"
- Description could be copy-pasted from a job posting and still make sense
- No numbers or specifics appear above the fold

**Phase:** Hero rewrite — address first, before any design work.

### Pitfall 2: Rotating Type Animation Dilutes the Role Signal

**What goes wrong:** The TypeAnimation cycling through "Help Desk Support Specialist" → "IT Service Desk Professional" → "Technical Support Engineer" → "Microsoft 365 Troubleshooter" feels dynamic but creates a problem: the visitor's brain never locks in a clear role identity. The animation looks polished but communicates uncertainty. A recruiter scanning the page for 5 seconds will catch whatever word is mid-animation.

**Why it happens:** Rotating text is a popular pattern because it signals range. The assumption is that covering more terms improves discoverability or appeal. In practice, for a job-seeking portfolio, it fragments the single most important signal a recruiter needs: who is this person?

**Consequences:**
- The visitor cannot answer "what does this person do?" from a glance.
- On mobile, the animation is even harder to read mid-transition.
- Four near-synonymous IT support titles reinforce "support generalist" when the site should be projecting a clearer identity.

**Prevention:**
- Pick one precise title and commit to it. The role that best matches the target job or the most senior realistic target.
- If the candidate is targeting multiple audiences (IT support vs. sysadmin vs. junior DevOps), the title should be the pivot point, not the animation. A subtitle line or tagline can handle nuance without sacrificing clarity.
- Reserve animation for something that adds information, not repeats it — a metric that counts up, a status badge, a skill ribbon.

**Warning signs:**
- The four rotating titles are near-synonyms (all mean "IT support")
- No one title would confidently appear on a business card

**Phase:** Hero rewrite.

### Pitfall 3: Stats Without Context Are Noise

**What goes wrong:** The hero metrics strip shows `8+ YRS EXPERIENCE`, `20+ STAFF SUPPORTED`, `120+ USERS / WEEK`. These are numbers, but they are not impressive claims — they are descriptions. "20+ staff supported" is ambiguous and small-sounding. "120+ users / week" lacks comparison (is that a lot? At what kind of organisation?).

**Why it happens:** Numbers feel credible, so candidates add them. But a number without context does not trigger the "wow" response — it prompts "is that good?"

**Consequences:** The stats strip takes up prime hero real estate and delivers low persuasive return. A recruiter who spots "20+ staff supported" may actually downgrade their estimate of the candidate's scope.

**Prevention:**
- Every stat needs either implicit or explicit scale context. "120 users / week across 3 sites" is better. "99.2% first-call resolution rate" is better than "120+ users."
- The most powerful stats are the ones that replace a pain point the hiring manager has. Not "how busy you are" but "what breaks when you are not there."
- Three stats is right; the content of those stats needs to be chosen for impact, not just accuracy.

**Warning signs:**
- Any stat below 1,000 needs very strong context to be impressive
- Stats describe activity (how busy) rather than outcomes (what improved)
- No stat references a business impact

**Phase:** Hero rewrite — stats need to be reconsidered alongside the value proposition, not treated as decoration.

### Pitfall 4: Section Order Buries Credibility

**What goes wrong:** The current order is: Hero → About → Experience → Projects → Case Studies → Tools → Certifications → Achievements → Testimonials → Contact. The most credible sections (Case Studies, Achievements, Testimonials) are deep in the scroll journey. A recruiter who bounces at the 30% scroll mark never sees the strongest evidence.

**Why it happens:** Sections get added as the site grows and land wherever they fit. The About section feels like a natural "introduction" so it goes first, but recruiters already know the introduction is in the hero — they want proof, not biography, next.

**Consequences:** The scroll journey front-loads soft content (About, which is another description of the candidate) before hard evidence. By the time a recruiter reaches Testimonials or Case Studies, most have already formed an opinion and many have left.

**Prevention:**
- Sequence sections by credibility weight for the primary audience (recruiters/hiring managers): Hero → Evidence → Experience → Story → Contact.
- Move Achievements and Testimonials up. These are social proof — their job is to catch the visitor before they bounce.
- About works better after the visitor has seen evidence; it provides texture once curiosity is established.
- Suggested reorder: Hero → Achievements → Experience → Case Studies → Projects → Testimonials → About → Tools → Certifications → Contact.

**Warning signs:**
- Strong social proof (testimonials, quantified achievements) appears after the 60% scroll mark
- About section is position 2 in the page order

**Phase:** Section reorder — can be done independently of copy rewrites, low risk, high leverage.

### Pitfall 5: Design Distinction Without Copy to Match

**What goes wrong:** The NOC theme is visually striking. The risk is that design complexity compensates for weak copy — the visitor is impressed by the aesthetic but cannot remember anything specific about the candidate after closing the tab. Design creates the impression; copy creates the memory.

**Why it happens:** Visual work gives immediate feedback (it looks good or it doesn't). Copy improvement is harder to evaluate and easier to defer. Redesign efforts often invest heavily in the visible layer and leave the words untouched.

**Consequences:** The portfolio is memorable as "that cool dark IT site" but not as "the engineer who reduced resolution time by X." The design wins style points; the copy should be winning recall and callbacks.

**Prevention:**
- Treat copy as a design material with equal weight to colour and layout. The words are the product.
- The design's job is to make the copy credible and trustworthy — not to distract from it.
- After any design upgrade, ask: if you stripped the visual design and read only the text, would the candidate still stand out?

**Warning signs:**
- The most recent redesign discussion focuses on animations, colours, and layout — not on what specific words to change
- The hero paragraph has not been touched since the last visual redesign

**Phase:** All phases — but especially the copy rewrite phase, which must not be subordinated to design work.

## Moderate Pitfalls

---

### Pitfall 6: "Soft Skills" as a Section or List

**What goes wrong:** Listing "Problem Solving, Technical Documentation, User Training, Team Collaboration, Communication, Adaptability" as a distinct skills block (as in the current `AboutSection`) adds nothing. Every candidate lists these. They are expected, not differentiating. The current six soft skills are identical to what you would find on any junior-to-mid IT CV.

**Prevention:**
- Remove the soft skills list entirely. Let the case studies and experience descriptions demonstrate these qualities through narrative rather than assertion.
- "Communication" as a bullet point is noise. A testimonial that says "Peter explained a complex network issue to a board-level exec in five minutes" is evidence.

**Warning signs:** Any list that contains "Team Player," "Communication," or "Adaptability" as standalone items.

**Phase:** About section rewrite.

---

### Pitfall 7: Over-Labelling With NOC Jargon

**What goes wrong:** The NOC aesthetic uses technical labels (HOSTNAME: PETER-WK, 192.168.1.5 // ACTIVE, eth0, eth1) as visual decoration. Carried too far into the copy layer, this reads as cosplay rather than authenticity. When decorative labels outnumber substantive content elements, the design starts to feel like a theme rather than a genuine identity.

**Why it happens:** The aesthetic is coherent and fun to build. Each new NOC detail feels like it reinforces the theme. There is a tipping point where the aesthetic overwhelms the content.

**Consequences:** Non-technical recruiters (common in IT hiring pipelines) may find the interface confusing or trying too hard. Technical hiring managers may notice that the labels are decorative (eth0/eth1 on a personal workstation, for instance) and it slightly undermines credibility.

**Prevention:**
- Keep NOC decoration in the SVG diagram and structural chrome (borders, grid lines, status LEDs). Do not let it spill into body copy or replace real content.
- Any label that does not communicate real information should be either removed or made clearly decorative.
- The test: if you were a non-technical HR recruiter, would the decorative labels confuse or impress you?

**Warning signs:**
- Technical labels appear in hero copy alongside the value proposition
- A visitor not familiar with networking would misread a label as content

**Phase:** Design boldness upgrade — establish which decorative elements add vs. subtract from the message.

---

### Pitfall 8: CTA Copy That Defaults to Lowest-Friction Phrasing

**What goes wrong:** "Download CV" and "Get in Touch" are safe, standard, and forgettable. They work but do not reinforce the brand or urgency. On a site this visually distinctive, generic CTA copy is a missed opportunity.

**Prevention:**
- CTAs can carry voice without adding friction. "View My CV" → "Download CV — PDF" is a functional improvement. "Get in Touch" → "Start a Conversation" or "Reach Out" is marginally better but still generic.
- The primary CTA for a job seeker should be oriented toward the recruiter's action, not the candidate's offer. "Book a call" or "Request CV" frames the recruiter as the actor.
- Do not over-engineer this — clarity trumps cleverness for CTAs. But review whether the phrasing matches the site's voice.

**Phase:** Copy rewrite — low-effort adjustment, do not block on it.

---

### Pitfall 9: Achievements Section With Unanchored Numbers

**What goes wrong:** Animated number counters (common in achievement sections) impress visually but require the numbers to be anchored to a context. "2000+ tickets resolved" needs "over 3 years, maintaining a 94% satisfaction score" to be meaningful. A raw number on its own can be dismissed as inflated.

**Prevention:**
- Each achievement metric needs a denominator, timeframe, or comparative anchor.
- Prefer relative claims when absolute numbers are small: "Reduced average resolution time by 40%" reads stronger than "Average resolution time: 18 minutes."

**Phase:** Achievements section review — treat alongside the hero stats rewrite.

## Minor Pitfalls

---

### Pitfall 10: Tools Section as a Logo Grid

**What goes wrong:** A grid of tool logos or names tells the visitor what the candidate has touched, not what they can do with it. "Docker" as a list item is very different from "containerised the deployment workflow, cutting environment setup time from 2 hours to 8 minutes."

**Prevention:** Keep the tools grid for scannability (recruiters use it as a keyword check), but pair at least some entries with a one-line impact claim. Even a tooltip would do.

**Phase:** Low priority; fix after hero and copy rewrite are done.

---

### Pitfall 11: CV Download Is a `.txt` File

**What goes wrong:** The hero CTA downloads `cv.txt`. The download attribute is set to `Peter_Williams-Key_CV.pdf` — a mismatch that may confuse users when they receive a .txt file their OS opens in a text editor. This undermines the professional presentation at the moment a recruiter decides to take action.

**Prevention:** Replace `cv.txt` with a properly formatted PDF. Ensure the `download` attribute filename matches the actual file extension.

**Warning signs:** The file at `/public/cv.txt` is a plain text file and will not open as a PDF.

**Phase:** Should be resolved before any other improvements — it is a live embarrassment on the current site.

## Phase-Specific Warnings

| Phase Topic | Likely Pitfall | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Hero rewrite | Leading with description, not value (Pitfall 1) | Write the value prop before any other copy; test by reading aloud without the visual design |
| Hero rewrite | Keeping rotating type animation (Pitfall 2) | Commit to one title; reassign the animation slot to something that adds information |
| Hero rewrite | Retaining weak stats (Pitfall 3) | Audit each stat for business impact; replace activity metrics with outcome metrics |
| Section reorder | Soft content appearing before hard evidence (Pitfall 4) | Map sections by credibility weight for recruiters; evidence first |
| Design boldness | Visual upgrade without copy upgrade (Pitfall 5) | Run copy and design upgrades in the same phase, not sequentially |
| About rewrite | Soft skills list (Pitfall 6) | Delete the list; let case studies carry the proof |
| Design boldness | Over-decoration with NOC jargon (Pitfall 7) | Audit decorative labels against a "non-technical recruiter" comprehension test |
| Copy polish | Generic CTAs (Pitfall 8) | Review CTA wording last; low priority compared to body copy |
| Achievements | Naked numbers without anchors (Pitfall 9) | Every metric needs a timeframe or comparative anchor |
| Pre-launch | CV file is a .txt served as .pdf (Pitfall 11) | Fix immediately — this is live and breaks the primary CTA |
